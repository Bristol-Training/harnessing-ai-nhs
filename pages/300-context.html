Context matters

Key Takeaways
  • Context is all the information you give LLMs to understand your request (role, purpose, audience, requirements)
  • Be specific, not vague - Clear context gets relevant responses; vague prompts get generic ones
  • Refine iteratively - Start basic, review output, add details, repeat until satisfied
  • Never share sensitive data - No personal records, unpublished research, or confidential information

Large Language Models work by navigating through vast embedding spaces—multidimensional representations of knowledge and concepts. Vague or poorly defined context can lead the model to explore irrelevant areas of this space, producing generic or off-target responses. Well-crafted context act as precise navigation instructions, guiding the model to the most relevant knowledge areas and ensuring outputs that match your specific needs and context.

Why Context Matters for LLMs

Context is everything you provide to an LLM to help it understand your request and generate appropriate responses during your chatbot session. Without proper context, LLMs often produce generic, inaccurate, or inappropriate outputs.

Because LLMs have no memory between separate sessions, they have no inherent knowledge about who you are, your organization’s policies or the purpose of your request.

Example of poor context

Write me a report about productivity

Example of better context

I am a manager at the North Bristol NHS Trust. I have to write a 2-page executive summary about remote work productivity for the Senior Management Team, focusing on evidence-based strategies and including practical implementation steps.”

Note that while LLMs have no memory between separate chat sessions and don’t retain information from previous conversations, the data you share within each individual session may still be stored by the service provider. Always follow University’s data protection policies when sharing any information.

AI Policy at NBT & UHBW (HG-IG-001)

Independently of the prompting framework you are using, never share these with unapproved or general-purpose AI tools:

🚫 Personal Identifiable Data (PID): Patient medical records, health status, NHS numbers, or staff personal files.
🚫 Sensitive Clinical Information: Diagnostic scans, consultation transcripts (from AI Scribes), or unverified discharge summaries.
🚫 Confidential Medical Research: Unpublished clinical trial findings, grant applications under review, or proprietary drug discovery data.
🚫 Commercially Sensitive Data: Trust procurement contracts, vendor partnership agreements, or financial budgets.
🚫 Legal & Security Sensitive: Legal advice regarding clinical negligence, disciplinary proceedings, passwords, or system access credentials.

Types of Context

Explicit Context. Information you directly provide to the LLM, for example:

  • Your role and organization
  • The purpose of the task
  • Target audience

Implicit Context. Assumptions the LLM makes based on your prompt, for example:

  • Cultural assumptions
  • Educational level expectations
  • Language formality
Making Implicit Context Explicit

Instead of assuming the LLM will understand your context, state it clearly:

Instead of

Help me write an application for a fellowship

Try

I am a Specialty Registrar in cardiology applying for a MRC Clinical Research Training Fellowship. Help me draft a 3‑page Case for Support and a 1‑page Training & Development Plan tailored to this scheme.

Working Within Context Limits

LLMs have context windows, limits on how much text they can process at once or over different iterations. Some practical strategies to overcome this limitation are:

  • Summarize lengthy background information and prioritize the most important context
  • Break complex tasks into smaller parts
  • Use previous outputs as context for follow-up requests

Iterative Context Building

We will not always get what we want at the first attempt. A useful strategy is starting with basic context and refine:

  1. Initial request: Provide core context
  2. Review output: Identify what’s missing or wrong
  3. Refine context: Add specific details or corrections
  4. Iterate: Repeat until satisfactory

Patient: [SAMPLE PATIENT]
Age: 45F
Date: 03/01/2026

Presenting Complaint

6-month history of progressive fatigue, unintentional weight gain (4kg), and cold intolerance. Also experiencing hot flashes x 8 months which she initially attributed to perimenopause.

History of Presenting Complaint

Symptoms started gradually last summer with hot flashes occurring 4-5 times daily. Over subsequent months developed:

  • Persistent fatigue not relieved by rest
  • Weight gain despite no dietary changes
  • Cold intolerance, particularly hands and feet
  • Dry skin and thinning, brittle hair
  • Constipation (bowels now every 3-4 days, previously daily)
  • Brain fog - feels “slowed down” mentally, colleagues have noticed changes in productivity

No neck pain, dysphagia, voice changes, or recent viral illness.

Past Medical History

  • Hypercholesterolemia (diagnosed 2018) - managed with atorvastatin 20mg
  • Otherwise well

Medications

  • Atorvastatin 20mg OD
  • Paracetamol PRN for headaches

Allergies: NKDA

Family History

  • Sister diagnosed with Graves’ disease aged 38 (significant for autoimmune thyroid disease)
  • Mother: T2DM, hypertension
  • Father: IHD

Social History

Non-smoker, alcohol 6-8 units/week. Works as secondary school teacher. Lives with husband and two teenage children.

Examination

Vital Signs:

  • BP 128/82 | HR 58 bpm (regular) | Temp 36.4°C
  • Weight 74kg (previous 70kg six months ago) | BMI 27.3

Thyroid Examination:

Small, diffusely enlarged thyroid gland, non-tender with smooth texture. No discrete nodules palpable. No lymphadenopathy or bruit.

Other Findings:

  • Delayed relaxation phase of ankle reflexes (typical hypothyroid sign)
  • Dry skin noted on extremities
  • Bradycardia
  • CVS and respiratory exam otherwise unremarkable

Investigations (28/12/2025)

Thyroid Function Tests:

  • TSH: 12.4 mIU/L (0.4-4.0) ↑↑
  • Free T4: 8.2 pmol/L (9-25) ↓
  • Free T3: 3.1 pmol/L (3.5-6.5) ↓

Thyroid Antibodies:

  • Anti-TPO: 486 IU/mL (<35) ↑↑↑ - strongly positive
  • Anti-thyroglobulin: 152 IU/mL (<40) ↑

Liver Function Tests:

  • ALT: 67 U/L (10-40) ↑
  • AST: 58 U/L (10-40) ↑
  • ALP, bilirubin, albumin, GGT - all within normal limits

Lipid Profile:

  • Total Cholesterol: 6.8 mmol/L (elevated despite statin therapy)
  • LDL: 4.2 mmol/L | HDL: 1.6 mmol/L | Triglycerides: 2.2 mmol/L

Full Blood Count:Normal (Hb 128 g/L, WCC 6.8, platelets 245)

Renal Function & Electrolytes: Normal (Cr 68, eGFR >90, Na 139, K 4.2)

Glucose Metabolism: Normal (fasting glucose 5.2 mmol/L, HbA1c 36 mmol/mol)

Assessment

Primary hypothyroidism secondary to Hashimoto’s thyroiditis

This patient presents with classical hypothyroid symptoms (fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, cognitive slowing, bradycardia) confirmed biochemically with elevated TSH and reduced thyroid hormones. The markedly elevated anti-TPO antibodies (>10x upper limit) strongly support Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Positive family history of autoimmune thyroid disease (sister with Graves’) and diffusely enlarged smooth thyroid gland are consistent with this diagnosis.

The mildly elevated transaminases are likely related to hypothyroidism itself - this commonly causes hepatic dysfunction that typically resolves with thyroid hormone replacement. Similarly, the elevated cholesterol despite statin therapy is a known consequence of untreated hypothyroidism.

Hot flashes are likely perimenopausal symptoms occurring concurrently, though will reassess once euthyroid as thyroid dysfunction can occasionally affect temperature regulation.

Management Plan

1. Thyroid Hormone Replacement

Starting levothyroxine 50 micrograms once daily. Using lower starting dose given cardiovascular risk factors (hypercholesterolemia). Advised to take on empty stomach 30 minutes before breakfast, avoiding calcium/iron supplements within 4 hours.

2. Monitoring Strategy

  • Recheck TFTs in 6-8 weeks (TSH and Free T4)
  • Aim for TSH in lower half of reference range (0.4-2.5 mIU/L)
  • Titrate dose in 25 microgram increments based on results
  • Recheck LFTs at 3 months to confirm improvement
  • Reassess lipid profile once euthyroid - may be able to adjust statin dose

The patient’s mildly elevated liver enzymes and cholesterol are likely secondary to hypothyroidism and are expected to improve with treatment. Hot flashes may be related to perimenopause but will be reassessed once thyroid function normalizes.

Contextualised prompt

We want to use a tone that is empathetic and use simple language to explain the medical condition and prognosis.

Context. I am a Specialist Registrar in an NHS outpatient clinic drafting a letter to the patient after today’s appointment. I will provide the model with structured clinical notes.

Purpose. Create a clear, empathetic, patient‑friendly letter that explains the diagnosis and likely prognosis, outlines the agreed plan, and gives practical next steps.

Audience. The patient (and their family/carers), using plain English and UK spelling. Assume no clinical background. Tone & Style. Warm, respectful, and reassuring. Avoid jargon; define any necessary medical terms. Target a reading level suitable for the general public. Use short sentences and clear headings.

Safety & Governance. Do not invent facts; only use information present in the notes. Flag uncertainty transparently.

Privacy. Do not include sensitive details beyond what is needed to communicate care.

Format. One page. Include:

  • Sections: “What we discussed today”, “Your diagnosis explained”, “What this means for you”, “Your treatment and monitoring plan”, “Follow‑up and contacts”.
  • Bullet points for actions the patient should take.

Output. Return only the drafted letter content (no meta commentary).

Dear [Patient Name],

What we discussed today

You told me about feeling very tired for several months, gaining weight without changing your diet, feeling cold, and having dry skin and constipation. You also mentioned hot flushes, which started earlier.

Your blood tests and examination helped us understand what is causing these symptoms.

Your diagnosis explained

You have a condition called hypothyroidism, which means your thyroid gland is not making enough thyroid hormone. This hormone controls energy, temperature, and metabolism.

The tests show this is due to Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, an autoimmune condition where your immune system attacks the thyroid gland. This is common and treatable.

What this means for you

With the right treatment, most people feel much better. The tiredness, weight changes, and other symptoms usually improve over weeks to months. This is a long-term condition, but it can be managed well with medication and regular monitoring.

Your treatment and monitoring plan

Start levothyroxine 50 micrograms once daily.

Take it on an empty stomach, at least 30 minutes before breakfast. Avoid taking calcium or iron supplements within 4 hours of your dose.

We will check your thyroid blood tests again in 6–8 weeks and adjust the dose if needed. We will also recheck your liver tests and cholesterol once your thyroid levels are stable.

Follow‑up and contacts

Blood test in 6–8 weeks (we will send you the details). Review appointment after your next blood test.

If you have questions or new symptoms, contact your GP or our clinic on [Clinic Phone Number].
